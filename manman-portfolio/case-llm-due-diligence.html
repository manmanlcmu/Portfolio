01 — The situation

The AI worked. Users still weren’t satisfied.

The engineering team had built an LLM-based prototype to automate pre-due-diligence analysis for investors evaluating target companies. It could produce analysis far faster, and across far more information, than a person could.

But early pilot users reported poor usability and a mismatch with how they actually work. My job was to understand their workflow and decision process, then redesign the experience so it supported investment decisions, within what the team could ship as an MVP in six sprints.

02 — What research showed

Without clear signals of uncertainty, users check everything.

The prototype presented AI output the way a report reads: sequential, complete, every finding equal. Investors don’t think that way. They start from a question, look for the answer, and spend their judgment where the evidence is weakest.

DECISION 01 · INPUT QUALITY

Structured outline input

The problem. Users started with an open-ended project description, so the model often worked from incomplete or inconsistent context.

The decision. A guided intake that captures five things before the model runs: the target, deal type, investment rationale, focus areas, and the hypotheses to test.

Why it matters. Better analysis starts before the model runs: complete, consistent context raises output quality for every step after it.

DECISION 02 · TIME TO INSIGHT

Conclusion first, evidence after

The problem. The prototype produced dozens of pages of analysis, laid out topic by topic. With no answer up front, it was hard for reviewers to build their own top-line chain of reasoning across that much material.

The decision. Every section opens with its own summary: the answer, the reasons behind it, and the key risks. Once all sections are done, they roll up into an overall executive summary.

Why it matters. In a long report, the summary gives users a storyline to follow. They read the detail to check the answer rather than to find it, so they stay oriented and review with a critical eye. It is how good analysts already communicate: answer first, then the reasons.

DECISION 03 · REVIEW EFFORT

Label how every claim was made

The problem. Every statement looked the same, whether it came straight from a source or from the model’s own reasoning. Users couldn’t tell what was grounded, so they re-checked everything with equal effort.

The decision. Mark each claim by how it was produced. Sourced facts stay plain text with a citation. Conclusions the model synthesized are labeled with their sources. Logical leaps and missing evidence get their own flags. Flags sit as highlights in the text, with the details in a side panel that reviewers can filter by impact.

Why it matters. Each label tells reviewers which check to run: trust a cited fact, compare a synthesis with their own interpretation, judge whether a leap is reasonable. Human judgment goes exactly where it is needed.

How the system tells claims apart

Asking a model to label its own sentences isn’t reliable: it rarely notices its own leaps. So I defined four types precisely, and made the labels come from checks rather than self-assessment.

Four types of information

S1

Fact

Stated directly in a source document.

How it’s detected

Matched to a source passage, then checked a second time that the passage really supports the claim.

Synthesized

Synthesized

A conclusion drawn by combining two or more sourced facts.

How it’s detected

Follows from the cited sources taken together, with every source listed.

Leap

Leap

A claim that needs a premise no source or earlier claim provides.

How it’s detected

Whatever is left: neither found in a source nor derivable from other claims.

Missing

Missing evidence

A question from the intake that the approved sources can’t answer: the user’s own data and designated authoritative sources.

How it’s detected

Each intake question is searched against the source pool before writing. If nothing relevant comes back, it’s flagged, never answered from the model’s own knowledge.

The hardest one: leaps

Leaps aren’t errors. Every investment call is a bet on the future. The problem was leaps hidden among facts.

So the model still reasons freely, but a bare “leap” label isn’t enough to review. Each one is grounded in three ways: what kind of leap it is, which premise it’s missing, and how much the section’s conclusion depends on it.

Four kinds of leap

Extrapolation

Past → future

“Grew for three years” → “will keep growing”

Causal

Correlation → cause

“Mostly repeat customers” → “growth comes from loyalty”

Generalization

Part → whole

“Top accounts are stable” → “the customer base is stable”

Context transfer

Today’s conditions → new ones

“Performs well today” → “will perform well after the deal”

Leap

“Growth should continue after the acquisition.”

Key trade-offs

What I chose not to do, and why

TRADE-OFF 01 · SCOPE

I prioritized the first generation over the editing loop.

Why

The first report a user sees shapes their trust in the system more than anything after it. If that output is hard to review, they never reach the editing stage.

What I gave up

When a user corrects a claim, the report needs to show the edit and re-check everything that depends on it before work continues. I left that out of the MVP.

TRADE-OFF 02 · SPEED

I prioritized time to first insight over a complete summary up front.

Why

An overall executive summary needs every section generated first, which could take more than 30 minutes. So each section opens with its own summary and appears within minutes; the overall summary follows once all sections are done.

What I gave up

Users don’t see the full top-line view at the start. Their first read builds the storyline section by section.

TRADE-OFF 03 · NOISE

I chose to flag comprehensively over flagging selectively.

Why

Deciding which flags to hide would add another abstract judgment for a model that was still maturing, and it would likely get it wrong. And if a hidden issue surfaced later, users would lose trust: “I checked everything it showed me, so why is this still wrong?”

What I gave up

A busier page and more to review. I eased it without hiding anything: flags sit in a side panel, and users can filter by impact while everything stays flagged.

Keeping the model honest

How I evaluated the model

I judged the model on the errors that would cost a reviewer most, not on generic accuracy. Together they answer one question: does the model stay honest about its evidence?

Criterion

Why it matters

How I measured

Citation support

A fact is only trustworthy if its source really says it.

Experts checked whether each cited passage actually supports its claim.

Leap recall

A leap passed off as a fact is the costliest error a reviewer can miss.

Share of expert-identified leaps that the system flagged.

Label accuracy

A wrong label sends the reviewer to the wrong kind of check.

Agreement between the system’s labels and expert labels.

Honest gaps

When evidence is missing, the model must say so, not fill the gap with a plausible guess.

Experts reviewed every gap in the sources and checked whether the model flagged it or filled it in.

Test set

Domain experts annotated generated reports claim by claim as the ground truth. Corrections users made during the pilot were collected and analyzed with the team’s AI researcher to extend it.

The bar

Leap recall came before precision. Over-flagging costs a reviewer seconds; a missed leap can cost a deal.

When the numbers fall short

Fix from cheapest to most expensive, and only move on when the cheaper fix runs out.

01

Diagnose

Sort every failure by cause: retrieval missed it, the model misread it, or the reasoning jumped.

02

Fix the prompt

Tighter instructions and structured output, e.g. no citation, no claim.

03

Fix retrieval

Better chunking, source quality and ranking. Many citation errors start here.

04

Add checking agents

A separate agent verifies each claim against its source before it reaches the user.

05LATER

Reinforcement learning

Considered for later. It needs far more data and investment than the steps above.

Where errors come from

Corrections users make while working, plus expert spot checks that catch what users don’t notice. Every confirmed error becomes a test case the next version must pass.

Process — How I got there

From observed pain points to a validated MVP flow.

Every design decision traced back to how users actually worked, and was tested with them before engineering committed to it.

Expert users like these are few, and their time is scarce. So I went deep rather than wide: the same participants from first interview to final test, which made every comparison direct.

STEP 1

Understand

Interviewed corporate finance and VC professionals on their end-to-end workflow, and watched them use the prototype to find pain points.

STEP 2

Define the flow

Mapped the current journey and decision steps, then defined the AI-assisted workflow as user stories with a UX designer.

STEP 3

Prototype

Built a clickable prototype in Stitch and Figma, structuring AI output around the decisions users make.

STEP 4

Validate

Tested the new flow with the same users on key tasks, so feedback compared directly against the prototype. The new UX was approved for the MVP.

What I’d carry forward

1

Design for how people think, not just what they do

The prototype mirrored users’ steps: enter information, generate, check. The real job was cognitive: grasping a huge body of information and staying on top of its main line of argument. Structuring the output around that is what made it usable.

2

Measure the cost of review, not just the cost of generation

An AI feature is only as valuable as the time it saves after people check its work. If review effort eats the speed gain, the product hasn’t delivered, however good the model looks.

3

Let the model prioritize, never let it hide

When error costs are asymmetric, decide which mistake you can least afford, then build and evaluate around it. A mis-ranked flag costs seconds; a hidden one costs trust.

4

Earn trust on the first output, then build the rest

Users decide whether to rely on an AI system early. Sequencing scope around the first experience, and how fast it delivers insight, mattered more than covering the full journey in v1.
